My research sits at the intersection of software engineering, artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, geospatial information science, and data governance. I am particularly interested in research that turns an important real-world problem into a useful system, method, or evidence-based design.

The projects below are examples of the kinds of topics that can be developed into Master's or PhD research. A project would normally be narrowed to a clear research question, grounded in the relevant literature, implemented or investigated systematically, and evaluated with appropriate technical, human, privacy, security, or community measures. The final scope depends on the student's background, interests, programme requirements, supervision availability, ethics approval, and access to data or partner organisations.

<h2 class="text-primary">Current research themes</h2>

- **Geospatial privacy and Indigenous data sovereignty:** protecting sensitive location data while preserving enough information for research, planning, and community decision-making.
- **Responsible AI and student wellbeing:** designing culturally grounded AI systems that support learners while keeping people, safety, and human escalation at the centre.
- **Cybersecurity and human-centred security:** understanding technical vulnerabilities, cyber resilience, social engineering, and the ways people interact with secure systems.
- **Software engineering and open-source communities:** using empirical software engineering, data mining, and AI/NLP to understand how developers make decisions and collaborate.

<h2 class="text-primary">Possible Master's and PhD project directions</h2>

<h3 class="text-primary">1. Capture-time geospatial privacy and Indigenous data sovereignty</h3>

Field data can become sensitive as soon as it is collected. A strong project direction is to build privacy into the point where observations are captured, before they cross a device, organisational, or community trust boundary.

Possible directions include:

- **MapSafe Mobile:** an offline-first mobile GIS tool that applies masking, aggregation, local encryption, and controlled sharing on the device.
- **Progressive disclosure:** comparing original, donut-masked, H3-aggregated, and encrypted representations for different recipients and use cases.
- **Secure multi-party sharing:** investigating public-key or multi-recipient encryption, recipient management, and fine-grained access control.
- **Integrity and provenance:** recording how a location was transformed, who is authorised to use it, and whether a shared record has been altered.
- **Indigenous and Māori data governance:** translating principles such as authority, consent, provenance, CARE, and community-led decision-making into practical geospatial workflows.

A project could combine system design with threat modelling, privacy–utility analysis, usability studies, performance testing, or scenario-based evaluation. Outputs might include a mobile, QGIS, or web prototype; an evaluation framework; an open-source contribution; and a thesis or research paper.

<h3 class="text-primary">2. Predictive geomasking, trajectory masking, and privacy–utility trade-offs</h3>

Geomasking changes the location or movement information that is released so that people, places, or activities are harder to identify. The research challenge is to reduce disclosure risk without destroying the value of the data.

Possible questions include:

- Can machine learning select a masking strategy or radius based on local geography, population density, or the sensitivity of a record?
- How do predictive geomasking methods compare with random, grid, donut, cloaking, swapping, planar Laplace, or generalisation approaches?
- How should privacy be measured for repeated or multi-user trajectories, where individual points may be safe but movement patterns reveal identity?
- What is the relationship between k-anonymity, uniqueness, re-identification risk, spatial displacement, travel-time distortion, and analytical utility?
- Can a practical privacy-rating system help data custodians choose an appropriate release level?

These projects are well suited to machine learning, spatial statistics, synthetic-data experiments, benchmarking, and empirical evaluation using real or carefully constructed datasets.

<h3 class="text-primary">3. Responsible AI for student wellbeing and culturally grounded support</h3>

AI-based wellbeing systems raise important questions about personalisation, cultural fit, safety, privacy, and the boundary between automated support and human care. This direction builds on work involving trainer-gated, multi-persona conversational systems for student wellbeing.

Possible directions include:

- designing personas that reflect different forms of learner, whānau, peer, or community support;
- personalising language, tone, guidance, and escalation without stereotyping or over-automating care;
- creating human-in-the-loop workflows in which trained staff can review, guide, or interrupt responses;
- evaluating culturally safe interaction for students in Aotearoa New Zealand or other Pacific contexts;
- studying how students understand, trust, and appropriately use an AI wellbeing assistant.

Projects could use design science, co-design, interviews, scenario-based evaluation, NLP/LLM techniques, usability studies, and mixed methods. They should be framed as supportive technology rather than clinical diagnosis, with clear safety, consent, privacy, and referral boundaries.

<h3 class="text-primary">4. Cybersecurity, human-centred security, and cyber resilience</h3>

There are opportunities for Master's and PhD projects that connect secure technical implementation with the behaviour and decision-making of people and organisations.

Possible directions include:

- AI-assisted phishing and social-engineering detection, including cognitive and behavioural manipulation;
- anomaly detection, log analysis, and automated incident-response support;
- vulnerability analysis, ethical hacking, secure system design, and network or application security;
- responsible use of generative AI in security operations and software development;
- DevSecOps practices that make security measurable across the software lifecycle;
- human-centred studies of security warnings, authentication, privacy decisions, and cyber-resilience training.

Depending on the question, a project might use threat modelling, secure implementation, cyber-range or laboratory experiments, controlled user studies, interviews, surveys, or empirical analysis of security data.

<h3 class="text-primary">5. AI, data science, and NLP for software and open-source communities</h3>

Open-source projects produce rich records of requirements, discussions, decisions, code changes, and social relationships. These records can be studied to understand how software communities work and how their decision-making might be supported.

Possible directions include:

- using NLP or large language models to identify the rationale behind software decisions;
- analysing sentiment, influence, participation, roles, and social connections in less formally organised communities;
- mining requirements, issue discussions, or review conversations to identify risks and emerging needs;
- evaluating whether AI-generated summaries preserve the reasoning and minority viewpoints in a discussion;
- combining repository mining with interviews or surveys to validate what automated methods find.

These projects suit students interested in AI/data science, NLP, empirical software engineering, repository analysis, social network analysis, and mixed-methods research.

<h2 class="text-primary">How a project is developed</h2>

A typical project moves through the following stages:

1. Define a focused research problem, its users or communities, and the ethical or governance context.
2. Review the literature and identify a gap that can be addressed within the degree timeframe.
3. Design and implement a prototype, algorithm, dataset, study, or evaluation method.
4. Evaluate the result using measures appropriate to the topic, such as privacy, utility, security, usability, performance, cultural fit, or learning outcomes.
5. Communicate the contribution through a thesis, paper, software artefact, dataset, method, or practical guidance.

<h2 class="text-primary">Methods and technical areas</h2>

Depending on the project, students may work with mobile GIS, Android, QGIS, Web GIS, spatial databases, cryptography, threat modelling, cybersecurity laboratories, machine learning, NLP/LLMs, data mining, social network analysis, empirical software engineering, interviews, surveys, co-design, usability testing, and mixed-methods evaluation.

<h2 class="text-primary">For prospective students</h2>

Students from computing, software engineering, cybersecurity, data science, GIScience, information systems, or related backgrounds are welcome to get in touch. An initial enquiry is most useful when it includes:

- your academic or professional background and the programme you are considering;
- one or two themes from this page that interest you;
- the technical or research methods you already know;
- a possible research question, problem, or community you would like to work with; and
- your CV, academic transcript, and expected timeframe.

A project can be refined together to match the student's strengths and the needs of the research setting. Supervision, co-supervision, funding, data access, and project commencement are subject to availability, formal approval, and the requirements of the relevant programme.
